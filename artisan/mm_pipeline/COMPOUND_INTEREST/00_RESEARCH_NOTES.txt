1. THE POWER OF COMPOUND INTEREST (BASIC MATH)
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   $10,000 invested once at 8% annual return, no additional contributions:

     Year     Balance     Annual Interest Earned
       0      $10,000       —
      10      $21,589      $1,599
      20      $46,610      $3,452
      30      $100,627     $7,454
      40      $217,245     $16,092

   Interest earned: $207,245  |  Multiplier: 21.72×
   Source: lifecalculators.com.au — $10,000 at 8% for 40 Years

   KEY INSIGHT: In year 40, you earn more in interest ($16,092) than the
   entire original principal ($10,000). The money is doing the work.

2. THE RULE OF 72
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Years to double = 72 ÷ annual return rate

     Rate (%)    Years to Double
       4%           18.0
       6%           12.0
       8%            9.0
      10%            7.2
      12%            6.0

   Example: At 8%, your money doubles every 9 years.
   Over 40 years (~4.4 doublings), $10,000 → $217,245.

   Source: NerdWallet Compound Interest Calculator / Investor.gov Compound
   Interest Calculator

3. STARTING EARLY — THE AGE GAP
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Scenario A: Invest $5,000/year from age 25–34 (10 years, $50,000 total).
   Scenario B: Invest $5,000/year from age 35–44 (10 years, $50,000 total).

   Both assume 8% annual return. At age 65:

     Investor A (start 25): $776,856
     Investor B (start 35): $349,991

   Difference: $426,865 — despite putting in the SAME $50,000.
   The 10-year head start produced 2.2× more wealth.

   Source: 24/7 Wall St. — "Start Investing at 25 Instead of 35: The Math
   Shows You'll Have $426,000 More by 65" (May 2026)

   ALTERNATE FRAMING (Investopedia, 2025):
   Saver starting at 22 vs 32 vs 42, each contributing $8,400/yr:

     Start 22 (45 yrs compounding): ~2× balance of start 32
     Start 32 (35 yrs compounding): ~2× balance of start 42
     Start 42 (25 yrs compounding): ~¼ of start 22

   The earlier starter's money compounds for a decade longer. That decade
   is when the exponential curve steepens dramatically.

4. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY: WARREN BUFFETT
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - Began investing seriously at age 10.
   - Net worth at 30: ~$1 million ($9.3M inflation-adjusted).
   - By 50: ~$3.8 billion.
   - By 65: ~$81.5 billion.
   - Current (age 95): ~$132 billion.

   99% of Buffett's wealth was accumulated AFTER age 52.
   99.9% of his wealth would disappear if he stopped at 60, even at 22%
   annual returns.

   His average annual return: ~22%.
   Compare: Jim Simons (Renaissance Tech) compounded at 66%/year — but
   started at 50. His net worth: ~$23B (72% less than Buffett).

   KEY INSIGHT: Buffett's wealth is not about being the best investor by
   returns. It's about having the longest compounding runway.

   Sources:
     - CNBC, "Most of Warren Buffett's wealth came after age 65" (May 2024)
     - Morgan Housel, CNBC interview (2020)
     - Investopedia, "The Buffett Era as CEO Is Over" (Jan 2026)

5. THE IMPACT OF DIFFERENT RETURN RATES
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   $10,000 invested once, held for 30 years:

     Rate     Final Balance     Multiplier
      4%        $32,434          3.24×
      5%        $43,219          4.32×
      6%        $57,435          5.74×
      7%        $76,123          7.61×
      8%       $100,627         10.06×
      9%       $132,677         13.27×
     10%       $174,494         17.45×
     12%       $299,599         29.96×

   Going from 5% to 8% — a 3 percentage point difference — more than doubles
   your ending balance. Going from 8% to 12% triples it.

   KEY INSIGHT: Small differences in rate compound into enormous differences
   over time. The "average" investor who chases hot stocks (net of fees)
   significantly underperforms the buy-and-hold index investor.

   Source: lifecalculators.com.au — $10,000 at various rates for 30 Years

6. INFLATION — REAL VS NOMINAL RETURNS
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - Average annual S&P 500 return (since 1957): ~10.51% nominal
   - Average annual inflation (same period): ~3.5–4%
   - Average annual REAL return (S&P 500): ~6.64–6.81%

   Source: Investopedia — "S&P 500 Average Returns and Historical
   Performance" (2026); Dimensional Fund Advisors — "Will Inflation Hurt
   Stock Returns?" (2023)

   $10,000 invested at 8% nominal for 30 years = $100,627 (nominal).
   $10,000 invested at 5% real (8% − 3% inflation) for 30 years = $43,219
   (real purchasing power).

   KEY INSIGHT: Inflation is a hidden tax on returns. The headline number
   looks great; the real purchasing power gain is roughly 60% less depending
   on the period. Taxable accounts take another 15–30% off the top.

7. CASE STUDIES / ANALOGIES
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   7A. THE SNOWBALL ANALOGY (Warren Buffett)
       "Compound interest is like a snowball rolling down a very long hill.
       By the time it gets to the bottom, it is much larger. The trick is
       to have a very long hill, which means starting very young or living
       to be very old."

   7B. THE CHESSBOARD / RICE GRAINS ANALOGY
       If you place 1 grain of rice on chess square 1, 2 on square 2, 4 on
       square 3, doubling each square: by square 64, the total exceeds all
       the rice ever produced on Earth (~18.4 quintillion grains).
       This is the exponential nature of compounding.

   7C. THE TWO BROTHERS ANALOGY
       Brother A saves $200/month from age 25 to 45 (20 years), then stops.
       Total contributed: $48,000.
       Brother B saves nothing until age 45, then saves $500/month from
       45 to 65 (20 years). Total contributed: $120,000.

       At 8% return at age 65:
         Brother A: ~$589,000
         Brother B: ~$296,000

       Brother A contributed 60% LESS money but ended with 2× MORE.

8. MARKET CONTEXT
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - S&P 500 average annual return since 1928: ~10.09% nominal, ~6.81% real
   - S&P 500 average since 1957: ~10.51% nominal, ~6.64% real
   - 10-year Treasury (risk-free rate): typically 3–5% depending on period
   - Inflation (historical average): ~3.2%
   - Typical 60/40 portfolio long-term return: ~8–9% nominal
   - Typical balanced portfolio real return (after inflation): ~4–5%

   Sources: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern) historical returns dataset;
   Investopedia, Dimensional Fund Advisors

   CONTEXT NOTE: The 8% nominal assumption used in many examples is
   realistic for a 60–80% equity portfolio over long periods but should
   be caveated. 10–12% is aggressive for any balanced portfolio and
   reflects pure equity, pre-tax, pre-fee assumptions. 4–6% real is a
   more conservative planning assumption.

9. COMPOUNDING ENEMIES (ANTI-PATTERNS)
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - Fees: A 1% annual fee on a 30-year 8% portfolio reduces the final
     balance by ~25%. A 2% fee (common in actively managed funds) cuts
     it by ~45%.
   - Behavioral churn: Missing the 10 best days in the market over 30
     years can cut returns in half. Time in the market > timing the market.
   - Debt: Credit card debt at 22% APR is a guaranteed negative
     compounding machine. Pay it down before investing.
   - Sequence risk: Poor returns in the first 5 years of a portfolio have
     outsized long-term impact (especially relevant for lump-sum investors
     near retirement, less relevant for accumulators).
