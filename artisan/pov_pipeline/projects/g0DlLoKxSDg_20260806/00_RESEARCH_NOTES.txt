SECTION -1 — FORMAT MODE DECLARATION
------------------------------------------------------------
OUTPUT_MODE: A (Single-arc tragedy, six acts compressed for short-form)
Justification: A single arc lets one emotional spine — a boy who learned to draw light out of the dark being taught to put it out, then digging his way back — build one steady climb to a single peak and a mirror ending that snaps shut. A level-ladder would scatter the tunnel imagery across unrelated ranks and drain the retention mechanics inside 14 minutes. Single arc, six acts, one symbol, one wound that never heals.

ONE QUESTION BEFORE ANYTHING:
Who is this person, what visceral mistake trapped them, and what is the relentless physical pressure that drives the next 14 minutes?
ANSWER: A 21-year-old collier whose gift for hearing the earth makes him the finest listener in his seam — and exactly the man the Royal Engineers want to bury alive in the clay under France. His mistake: he trusted the army's promise that a good listener gets the safe galleries. The pressure: every day he counts another man's pick strokes through the clay and knows the roof owes him nothing.

### 0A — THE PROTAGONIST
You are a collier, twenty-one, in the Durham pit town of Marnley, 1916. Terraced rows, the chapel porch where the casualty lists are pinned, the winding engine that marks the hours. You work the B seam with your father, a deputy with three fingers missing from the pit fall that taught him never to trust a roof. Your mother holds the house together; your younger sister works the pit-bank screens, picking stone out of coal with hands black by dinner. You love Ellen Crowther, the apothecary's daughter, whose right hand is stained iodine-brown from the dispensary. Your closest friend is Joey Pickering, loud as the winding chain and broke by Thursday every week. Your one concrete dream: take the deputy's stripe under your father, marry Ellen, and put a second room on your mother's house with your own hands. The one loved object pre-war: a brass lamp-tally stamped with the family number seven, handed to you at thirteen when you passed your first lamp-check, with the words that now run your life.

THE TRIGGERING EVENT: In early 1916 the B seam floods and the owners board up the pit. Rather than join the infantry — where Durham battalions were already dying by the battalion — you and Joey volunteer for the new Royal Engineers tunnelling companies. A recruiting officer named Captain Saltmarsh tests you in a damp cellar: you name the roof fall before it moves, and he smiles. The physical consequence: your ears now belong to the army. The antagonist at the trigger: Saltmarsh, who stamps your number and posts you to a listening gallery.

MOTIVATION (non-banned): THE COERCED SPECIALIST, braided with THE HONOR-BOUND. Your skill traps you — the better you listen, the deeper they post you. And Joey volunteered first; a Harden does not let a man go down alone, and a collier does not say no to the dark.

THE IRONIC TRAIT: Your ear. It made you the best collier in the seam, and it is precisely why the war wants you in the quietest, most lethal place on the Western Front. The gift that keeps you alive is the sentence that keeps you buried.

THE CENTRAL WANT: To come home to Ellen with both your ears and both your hands still your own.

### 0B — THE IRONIC LOOP (one paragraph)
You set out to keep your ear and your hands for the future the pit owed you — the deputy's stripe, Ellen, the second room on your mother's house — and you end up spending the entire war underground, your hearing sharpened into a weapon the army aims at the enemy, the very gift that saved you in the seam now the thing that guarantees you a place in the clay. The final reveal is that the whole nightmare was set in motion by the first physical compromise: the day you accepted the post of listener, believing a man who is useful to the war will be kept out of it. That one acceptance is the hinge every later death swings on.

### 0C — THE CENTRAL SYMBOL
The brass lamp-tally stamped No. 7. A collier's identity — you trade it at the lamp room for your lamp and your life. Introduced Act 1. It appears in every act. Degrade path, one direction: handled in mud and fear, it wears smooth; in the Act 3 blast a hairline crack opens across the number and never closes; by Act 6 the seven is a ghost. The tally stays brass; you are what changes. Its meaning shifts exactly the way the source rivet's did — identity, then luck, then proof that your hands once drew light before the war taught them to put it out, then the thing you place in your son's palm.

### 0E — THE COST LADDER (one physical loss per act)
- ACT 1: The seam floods and takes the deputy's stripe with it. You walk out of the pit yard with your lamp in your hand and nothing to climb back to.
- ACT 2: You burn Ellen's best shirt — the one she made you — to bind a trapped clay-kicker's leg while Gray shouts at you to withdraw.
- ACT 3: You seal your own gallery with a camouflet and entomb the Lancashire soldier's photograph with the charge. The letter to his mother never goes; the address rots to mush in your pocket against your sweat.
- ACT 4: The hairline crack opens across the seven on your tally. On leave you give your greatcoat and your leave money to the widow of the boy you could not save, and go back to France in a shirt that does not keep the rain out.
- ACT 5: Joey's ears and fingers. Your own palms blister and split on fuse-twisting until you cannot feel the tally's edge, and you do not feel the cut in your palm that leaves rust-red smears on the brass.
- ACT 6: The bury takes your left ear — the ear you spent a lifetime training. The war literally removes the gift that put you in the clay. What is left is the hands, and the hands learn to hear.

### 0F — THE PIVOTAL SCENE PER ACT (choice between two deadly options)
- ACT 1: Choose between sinking with the boarded pit and marrying Ellen into nothing, or following Joey into the tunnellers where Durham men are already dying in numbers the town cannot count. Both feel like death. You go down.
- ACT 2: A clay-kicker is trapped under a soft fall while a German camouflet is going in. Choose between obeying Gray's order to withdraw and save the section, or digging out with bare hands toward the boy. You dig. The boy lives; you burn the shirt.
- ACT 3 (the cold open): Ear to the clay, the German rhythm closing. Choose between firing the camouflet, which will kill the man on the other side and seal your own crew's exit, or a silent withdrawal that lets the Germans lay their chamber under the infantry trench above. You fire. The ground lifts and you are still inside it.
- ACT 4: On leave in Marnley. Choose between staying — Ellen, the roof, the lie that the war is over — or going back to tunnels you swore you would never re-enter, because Joey is going back and Saltmarsh has already doomed the company. You go back.
- ACT 5 (Messines): The boy freezes in a collapsing gallery while the fuse is counting. Choose between dragging him out and risking the section and the mine, or leaving him to keep the deadline. You drag him. Then you stay with Joey through the counter-blast until the stretcher party comes — the beat the source earned by staying with Harry at the mine.
- ACT 6: Buried, the air dying. Choose between burning the candle to dig upward and swallowing the last oxygen, or holding still, tapping the three-knock code on timber, betting the section you stayed behind to save will come for you. You tap. They come.

### 0I — THE MID-POINT TWIST (MANDATORY)
TWIST_SEGMENT: NAR-024 (Act 4, the leave in Marnley — 50% mark)
WHAT BREAKS: The assumption that the army's officers will not waste men needlessly, and that the "safe gallery" you were promised at Lochnagar was ever safe. You believe you survived bad luck and that going home meant going home.
WHAT REPLACES IT: The darker reality — Captain Saltmarsh falsified the company's casualty returns so the tunnelling company could not be rotated home for the coming Messines mines. He knew for days, from a captured German map, that your gallery at Lochnagar sat directly over a German counter-chamber; he ordered the crew to keep digging to hit his deadline. The safe gallery was a lie the whole war has been built on.
WHO REVEALS IT: Sergeant-Major Gray, wounded in the final crater fighting and believing he is dying, tells you the truth in the transit hospital before you sail home. "I knew about the chamber, lad. The order was lawful. I followed it." He dies the next morning. The mentor who taught you the craft was part of the machinery that spends men.

### 0J — THE MIRROR ENDING (MANDATORY)

FINAL BODY LINE: "You press your ear to the clay of the new shaft and there is nothing to count at all — only water, rising the way water rises when you finally let it. And for the first time since the Somme, you stop counting."

SECTION 1 — TOPIC FOUNDATION
------------------------------------------------------------
Topic statement: The forgotten war under the war — the tunnelling companies of the Western Front, and the twenty-something colliers who spent four years buried alive so infantry could walk over the top. The story is a single man's tally: a Durham listener who counted enemy picks by heart.
Why this topic NOW: audiences are saturated with trench-top histories; the underground war is the one nobody has been shown. The imagery is instant — darkness, the scrape of a pick, the flame that flattens when the air dies — and it delivers the physical, claustrophobic stakes short-form videos are built on, without a single tank or beach landing.
Target audience hook anxiety: the fear of being buried alive, the nightmare of being unable to move while something gets closer, and the modern dread of being spent by people who keep numbers. The same anxiety that makes horror from a closet, made literal and historical. The viewer clicks because they want to know what it felt like to have your hearing turned into a weapon.

SECTION 2 — SETTING & WORLD RULES
------------------------------------------------------------
Setting: Marnley, a fictional pit town in the Durham coalfield, England, 1914-1919; then the Armentieres and Somme sectors, the Lochnagar line at La Boisselle, Messines Ridge, and a 1918 sector in Picardy. Two worlds: the town above ground (terraced rows, the winding engine, the chapel porch with the casualty lists) and the earth below (the seam, then the clay galleries).
Time period: the Great War, spring 1916 to the Armistice, with the mirror epilogue in 1919.
The unwritten rules of the world:
- The lamp room is a church. You trade your tally for your lamp and your life; a tally with a dead man's number is never re-issued.
- You never whistle in a gallery. A miner does not say "good luck" to a man going down. The dark is superstitious and it is watching.
- Under the Front, silence is not a courtesy, it is ammunition. Steel never touches steel near a listening post; boots are muffled; a wrong breath is a German borehole.
- The knock code is the only language the clay respects. One tap is a question. Two is danger. Three is you.
- The tunnel is the front line of the war where nobody can see it, and the officer with the map outranks the roof that is about to fall.
- What is said above ground about the tunnels is a lie. The papers say gallantry. The men say nothing.

SECTION 3 — SENSORY PROFILE PER ACT (Action-Focused)
------------------------------------------------------------
ACT 1 (The Pit): the smell of coal dust and lamp oil on the last clean shirt you own; the rattle of the winding chain marking the hours over the rows; the weight of the Davy lamp on its leather strap against your hip; the screen belt shifting stones behind the pit bank, a slow curtain that never stops; the sting of chapped knuckles in the winter water at the standpipe.
ACT 2 (Training, Armentieres): the smell of wet clay and sour sweat in a gallery six feet wide; the scrape of felt-muffled picks, a sound you learn to read like Morse; the weight of a waterlogged greatcoat that doubles your body; a candle flame leaning flat when the air thins, watched like a pulse; the raw burn of fuse-twisting across the palms at midnight.
ACT 3 (The Somme, Lochnagar): the smell of ammonal and burnt earth that clings to a throat for a week; the deep throat-shake of the mine going up under your boots; the weight of a soaked tunic after the crater fight, pulling your shoulders down; the ground trembling up through your soles like a heartbeat in the wrong place; the ringing in your left ear after the camouflet that will not stop until it becomes silence.
ACT 4 (Leave, Marnley): the smell of coal smoke and baking bread, which should be comfort and is only strange; the chapel bell, unfamiliar after the whistles, tolling names you half recognize; the weight of your father's hand on your shoulder, missing fingers and all; the pit-bank screens moving in the yard like a curtain across the town's grief; the ache of the tally's new crack under your thumb as you lie to your mother.
ACT 5 (Messines): the smell of cordite and crushed white chalk; nineteen muffled thumps counting under the ridge like a giant heartbeat, one after another, until the horizon lifts; the weight of Joey against your shoulder as you drag him through a collapsing gallery; the ridge visibly rising in the moonlight, an inch at a time; the tremor in your fingers as you arm fuses with blood on the brass.
ACT 6 (1918 and after): the smell of afterdamp and the ghost of gas in the rescue; the soft grit of soil sifting from the roof onto your face in the bury; the weight of the timber that pinned your shoulder; the candle guttering, then dying; the tight band of crushed ribs when the rescue picks finally reach you; and last — the clean mineral smell of water in a reopened seam, which is the smell of the war ending.

SECTION 5 — THE TOLL
------------------------------------------------------------
Real cost mapped to acts, framed as the official histories record:
- Recruitment and scale: by the end of the war the Royal Engineers fielded tens of tunnelling companies drawn overwhelmingly from British coalfields — the navy's loss was the army's gain, and the collier's body was a military asset. ACT 1.
- Casualties: the tunnelling companies took casualties at a rate that competed with the infantry, in darkness, from counter-mines, collapses, and afterdamp. Approximate figures from the official histories put several thousand tunnellers dead across the war; the companies that held the clay longest burned through men fastest. ACT 3 and ACT 6.
- The counter-mine arms race: on the Messines front in 1917 the British and German miners fought an underground war of chambers and camouflets; nineteen British mines fired on 7 June 1917 with roughly four hundred and fifty tons of ammonal, the largest man-made explosion of the war up to that date, reported as far away as London and Dublin. The ridge was taken in a day; the war continued for another year and a half. ACT 5.
- Burnout: the paranoia of the listening post — men who sat for hours counting strokes they could not see — produced the collapse of judgment the histories call tunnel strain; men who could not trust a roof again were rotated home or kept until they broke. ACT 2 to ACT 6.
- The medical bill: lung damage from dust and clay, chronic hearing loss, the amputations of the bury, and the hundreds of men whose bodies came up in pieces. By 1919 the term "shell shock" covered what the pits had always called the men the roof took. ACT 6.
- The town's bill: every pit town lost its young men to either the battalions or the shafts; the women who ran the pit bank and the brickworks did not un-learn it afterward. ACT 6 and the epilogue.

SECTION 6 — REAL CASES & UNWRITTEN RULES
------------------------------------------------------------
Real named individuals (kept as historical anchor; the protagonist, the named supporting cast, and the specific scenes are fictional):
- Sir John Norton-Griffiths MP, "Empire Jack" — the engineer and former soldier who lobbied the War Office in early 1915 and raised the tunnelling companies, drawing on colliers and clay kickers from the British coalfields. The reason a Durham listener exists in uniform at all. ACT 1.
- Captain William Hackett VC — a Royal Engineer sapper who, after a tunnel collapse at Givenchy in 1916, stayed with two wounded men and his own wounded crew instead of saving himself, and died with them when the gallery could not be reopened. His is the historical face of the stay-behind choice this story makes personal in Act 5 and redeems in Act 6. ACT 5 reference.
- General Sir Herbert Plumer — the commander who planned the Messines attack around the nineteen mines, and who said he did not know whether he was changing history or not, but he intended to change geography. The date-fixing career man who is the template Saltmarsh twists. ACT 5.
Unwritten rules, one per act:
- ACT 1 (the pit): "You never whistle in the seam, and a deputy never wishes a man good luck going down." To do it is to invite the roof.
- ACT 2 (the tunnels): "Near a listening post you do not strike steel on steel, you do not cough, you do not breathe like a man who is in a hurry." Silence is the ammunition.
- ACT 3 (the crater): "You never fire a mine while a friend is still forward of the lip." The order to wait is an order that can get you killed, and men wait anyway.
- ACT 4 (leave): "A man home on leave does not tell the town what the tunnels look like." The town reads the papers; the man watches the roof of his own mother's kitchen.
- ACT 5 (Messines): "Before a big firing there is no mail, no leave, and no man leaves the line." One word to the wrong ear and the enemy counter-mines under you.
- ACT 6 (after): "You never count the dead by name at the winding house." The names wait for the chapel porch, where the lists are pinned.
